They say you sell it once.
The books say the customer came back 82 Korean makers and importers of appliances, furniture, watches and bags · FY2025 audit reports · ₩10 of after-sales per ₩100 of advertising

Caff · November 2026
Data used — audit reports of externally audited Korean companies under household-goods (Korean Standard Industrial Classification, KSIC 2591, 2592, 4643, 4649) and appliance (2851, 2852) industry codes; 82 consumer-facing companies, FY2025. Itemized SG&A, provision notes and balance sheets parsed from the originals. Comparison groups are the populations of posts 15 and 16. Warranty and useful-life periods from the Consumer Dispute Resolution Standards (Korea Fair Trade Commission Notice 2025-14). Zero internal data.

Talk to anyone who sells appliances or furniture and the same sentence comes up: you sell it once. The person who bought a rice cooker buys the next one years later, and in between the company never sees them. So repeat-purchase rate is a yardstick that does not fit, and advertising buys a stranger every time. All true. But when I went through the audit reports, the customer was there, coming back. To get something fixed, to return it, to collect points. Of 82 companies selling household goods and appliances, 79 book advertising expense; 38 have at least one line in the books where a returning customer leaves a trace. In money terms: ₩10 of after-sales spending for every ₩100 of advertising. This piece is about what that ₩10 produces, why half the companies have no line for it at all, and how many years a company actually sees its own product once you take the law's own numbers.

01What we measured

We started with externally audited companies (those large enough to be legally required to file audited accounts) under the household-goods industry codes (KSIC 2591, 2592, 4643, 4649) and the appliance codes (2851, 2852). You cannot trust the codes alone. Codes 2591 and 2592 are named "metal forging and pressing" and "metal heat treatment and plating," and 505 of the 616 companies under them are OEM and B2B. So we began with the 92 that record advertising, sales commissions or promotion expense, meaning they sell to consumers, read the "company overview" paragraph of every audit report, and removed ten: one gold bullion dealer, five B2B metalworking and parts makers, one maker of laboratory instruments, and three sellers of consumables that get rebought monthly (two contact lens firms and a shampoo brand). Two companies that looked like a comparison app and a media outlet from the outside turned out, in their own overview, to make aluminum cookware and umbrellas. They stayed in. The audited entity is the unit.

That leaves 82: 34 appliance makers, 34 sellers of things you wear or carry (bags, watches, eyewear, cameras), 12 in furniture and home goods, 2 in kitchen metalware. Median revenue ₩32.2 billion, all fiscal 2025 audit reports. Read them as companies that sell things you keep at home or on your person for years.

From each report we read five things. Advertising expense and warranty expense from the itemized selling and administrative expenses; the warranty provision movement table in the notes (opening, additions, used, closing); refund and returns liabilities; customer loyalty (points) liabilities; and trade receivables from the balance sheet. We used an expense table only when its items summed to its total row, and a provision table only when opening plus additions minus usage equaled closing. Tables that did not reconcile were discarded.

02The money comes from the retailer, and the household is out of view

First, who pays these companies. Receivables divided by revenue times 365 gives a median of 30 days (interquartile 18 to 41). When a consumer pays by card the money arrives in days. A month means the buyer and the payer are different people. The money comes from dealers, hypermarkets, TV home-shopping channels and platforms; the household sits behind them. Only 20% collect in under 15 days.

This is not peculiar to the sector. Measured the same way, cosmetics come in at 30 days, food at 33, health supplements at 32. Only e-commerce is different, at 17. Every consumer business that sells through intermediaries has this blind spot. What differs is what happens next. Cosmetics get rebought in two months and food every week, but a rice cooker, a sofa or a watch comes round again years later. Businesses that see the customer again at the next purchase can match them up then. Here, that moment arrives once every several years.

Advertising runs at 3.4% of revenue (median, interquartile 1.8 to 6.2%): appliances 4.1%, bags and watches and eyewear 3.7%, furniture 1.8%. A little below the e-commerce 4.7% and cosmetics 4.3% measured in post 15. They do not spend less; they spend about the same. The difference is when they next see the person the advertising brought in.

03The three lines where a returning customer shows up

Accounting records a returning customer in three places, and all three are money going out.

The one who comes to get it fixed is the warranty provision: an estimate, from past experience, of what repairs under warranty will cost, with the year's actual repair spend leaving through the "used" column of the movement table. Companies that do not set up a provision expense each repair as warranty expense in SG&A. The one who brings it back is the returns or refund liability. The one who comes to collect is the loyalty program, the points liability. All three get a line only when the amount is material. So a missing line does not mean the thing does not happen. This piece says one thing only: is it visible from outside?

Size sorts it. Of the 12 companies above ₩100 billion in revenue, 9 have a line; of the 40 below ₩30 billion, 11. Median revenue is ₩18.9 billion where there is no line and ₩49.6 billion where there is. Small companies do not have fewer repairs; the amounts are too small to break out. Which means, read the other way, that the smaller the company, the more likely its returning customers are folded into some expense account and not counted even by the company itself.

The money that brings in a stranger is booked by 79 companies out of 82. The trace of a customer who came back exists at 38.

04In money terms: ₩10 of after-sales for every ₩100 of advertising

The amount actually spent on returning customers can be read at 15 companies: 11 that break out an after-sales line in SG&A (labeled warranty expense, quality assurance expense, after-service expense or service expense), and 4 whose provision movement table shows the year's usage. After-sales spend is 0.42% of revenue (median; bootstrap 95% interval [0.09, 1.13]), ranging from 0.02% to 3.21%. Against advertising expense the median is 10.0%, interval [1.5, 13.9]. For every ₩100 spent on advertising, ₩10 goes to the customer who came back for a repair. Eight of the 15 are at or above 10% and two are above 50%; both of those are appliances that need installation. Appliances alone, 10 companies, show a median after-sales ÷ revenue of 0.54%. One watch importer (K) spends 1.69% of revenue on after-sales and books the provision charge not in SG&A but in the cost of repair sales. Fixing things is a business there, not an expense.

You can also read how many years the books think the warranty lasts. Closing provision divided by the year's usage says how many years of repairs are sitting in the reserve. At the 6 companies where both additions and usage are legible it is 0.6 to 1.6 years, median 1.0. The 3 companies that state a warranty period in words say one year (two of them) or two years (one). The books hold one year's worth, and when the year is up, the customer drops off the balance sheet.

05What that ₩10 produces

The record that ₩100 of advertising produces is impressions, clicks and conversions. The platform knows who bought; the company receives an order number. This is the spot described in post 10, where the same person gets a different customer ID on every channel.

The record that ₩10 of after-sales produces is different. The person who comes to get something fixed gives a name, a phone number and an address; says which model and which serial number; when and where it was bought; what broke and how. A row the company could never buy with advertising appears in the service log. The person who brings it back says why. In this sector, returns are the only record with a "why" attached. The person who comes to collect points is saying they intend to come again.

Yet accounting has no line that records these people as people. Only lines for money going out. So after-sales becomes a cost-center number and the customer becomes a marketing number, and nowhere in the company is there a table that knows they are the same person. A filing can't confirm this. But when after-sales exists only as a single SG&A line, nothing in the structure forces the service log to be linked to order numbers. It was true at the companies we have seen as well.

₩100 of advertising calls in a stranger. ₩10 of after-sales receives someone who left their name, address, model and fault at the door. Accounting books both as money going out.

06The window the law gives you

How many years a company sees its own product is something the law has put a number on. The Consumer Dispute Resolution Standards (Korea Fair Trade Commission, KFTC, Notice 2025-14, in force from December 2025) set a warranty period and a parts-retention period for each product category, and the useful-life table tells you to use the parts-retention period as the product's useful life. In the law's eyes, then, the product lives in the home for the parts-retention period, and the company is responsible for it during the warranty period. Divide one by the other and you get the company's viewing window.

The window runs from 11% to 40%, and most of what this population sells sits at 11 to 20%. A company sees the household that bought a refrigerator for one year in nine, a rice cooker for one in six. That lines up with the books in §04 holding 1.0 year of reserve. The law says one year, the books hold one year, and after one year the customer is erased. For the next five to eight years that household does not exist for the company. On the day it buys again, what the company does is advertise. At the same price as calling in a stranger.

The window can be widened. Not by lengthening the warranty. There are ways to know where the product is after the warranty ends: paid repairs, consumables (filters, blades, baskets), product registration, service reminders. Each is a moment when the household walks into the company on its own, and what it takes is not money but one order-number column in the service log.

07So what do you do

This piece uses two yardsticks: after-sales spend ÷ advertising expense (money spent on customers who came back ÷ money spent to bring in new ones) and viewing window = warranty period ÷ useful life. Both come straight from the filings and the law.

08What this data cannot show

Case counts. Only money is visible. Whether ₩1.5 billion of after-sales is a thousand repairs or ten thousand, and how many households, appears in no filing. Without a cost per case the money cannot be turned into a count.

After-sales outside the warranty. Paid repairs after the warranty are revenue, and no account records them as a returning customer. A company like K that runs repairs through cost of sales keeps after-sales outside SG&A entirely. Outsourced repairs disappear into fees paid.

Returns through the channel. Returns via home-shopping channels and platforms stay in the channel's books and reach the company only as a deduction from revenue. The returns line is more common among bag, watch and eyewear sellers, probably because they sell more through their own stores and department-store counters and so take returns in their own records, but channel mix is not disclosed and we could not confirm it.

The 44 with no line. All that can be said is that their after-sales spend lies somewhere between 0% and 3.21% of revenue. At their median revenue of ₩18.9 billion, that is between zero and ₩610 million. That ₩610 million spread is as precise as public data gets on this industry: not invisible, but visible only to this degree.

The label "service expense". At 2 of the 11 (B and D) the after-sales line is called "service expense". It may include the running cost of a service center on top of repairs, so their after-sales ÷ advertising (51.3% and 13.8%) should be read as upper bounds. The other 9 are labeled warranty, quality assurance, product warranty or after-service expense.

Full resets. Three companies reverse the whole opening balance every year and set it up again. Their "usage" is an accounting reset, not repairs, so they were left out of the money sample. At the 8 companies whose returns movement table shows actual returns, the median return rate was 0.19%; with eight companies we did not use it as an argument.

09The choices, written down

Why consumer-facing only. Take all 616 by code and 505 OEMs enter with zero advertising, and you get the false finding that "companies that do not advertise earn more". That is exactly the mistake of measuring ODMs as brands in post 3.

Why SG&A warranty expense first, provision usage second. The SG&A line is also recorded by companies that hold no provision (they expense as incurred); usage exists only where a provision does. Both are "money that went to the customer who came back for a repair that year." We did not use additions to the provision. That is an estimate of customers who will come next year, not the ones who came that year.

Why contact lenses and shampoo were removed. Same industry codes, but products rebought monthly, so the premise "you sell it once" does not hold. Keeping them would add returns and points lines and nudge up the share of companies with a line (46%) a little, and that would not be a finding about this industry.

Why receivable days sit in §02. We first meant to use them as this sector's distinguishing feature. Measured with the same yardstick, other consumer goods came out the same. So it moved from a feature to a premise. The household is invisible to everyone; what differs is how long until you see it again.

10What this piece is trying to say

"You sell it once" is half right. The customer comes back: to get it fixed, to return it, to collect points. Companies are spending a tenth of their advertising on them, and the record that money produces is one that advertising cannot. But accounting records those people only as money going out, half the companies have no line for them at all, and by the law's own window a company sees its product for one year in five to one in nine of the time it spends in the home.

So in this industry customer records do not start from orders. It starts from the service log. Attaching one order number to repair intake, return reasons and consumable orders, and building the table that counts "households using our product", comes before any repeat-purchase rate. With that table, replacement demand comes back from someone you know rather than from an advertisement.

The hard part here comes before any repeat-purchase rate: joining service logs, returns and orders, written separately, into one household.

Making that join, so that the customer who came back becomes visible, is what we do.
