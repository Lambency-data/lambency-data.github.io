100 of profit on paper, 72 in the bank.
Which company changes every year 348 profitable consumer companies in five industries · 307 with two years · audit reports

Caff · October 2026
Data used — audit reports of externally audited Korean companies in cosmetics, food, beverages, health functional foods and e-commerce; 348 profitable companies, FY2025. Book profit (operating profit), cash into the bank (operating cash flow) and inventory/receivables movements read from the documents, plus last year's report for 307 companies with two years. Zero internal data.

"The company makes good money" means two different things. It can mean a lot of profit is written in the books, or it can mean a lot of cash came into the bank account. They sound the same, but they are not the same number. We put the two side by side for 348 profitable Korean consumer companies, straight from their audit reports. Companies with an operating margin above 12% wrote 100 of profit in the books and received 72 in the bank. Then we opened last year's reports and saw something stranger. The group does this every year, but the companies doing it this year were not the ones doing it last year.

01Two numbers: the books and the bank

An audit report tells the money story on two separate pages. The income statement records book profit (operating profit): what was sold, minus costs. The cash-flow statement records cash into the bank (operating cash flow): the money that actually came in and went out of the account from the year's business.

There are only two reasons the two differ. One is money not yet collected. Sell 100 million won to a retailer and the books say "sold 100 million" today, but the cash arrives on the retailer's settlement day. The other is goods not yet sold. Make 100 units and sell 60, and only 60 units of cost hit the books, while the money spent making the other 40 has already left the bank. Receivables and inventory. Whenever this piece says the books and the bank have parted, it means money is sitting in one of those two places.

Figure 1 · The two places where book profit stops short of the bank: money not yet collected (receivables) and goods not yet sold (inventory)

We measure both as ratios too. Book margin is book profit per 100 of sales (operating margin); bank margin is cash into the bank per 100 of sales (cash-flow margin). That is all the vocabulary this piece needs. From here on it is just the books and the bank.

Size first. Among externally audited companies in cosmetics, food, beverages, health functional foods and e-commerce, 348 recorded a profit. Of those, 68 (20%) had cash going out of the bank in the very year the books showed a profit. One in five.

Figure 2 · Of 348 profitable companies, 68 saw cash leave the bank in the year the books showed a profit

In the three bands under 12%, the books and the bank say the same thing: around 100. Cross 12% and it drops to 72. Take each company's book margin minus its bank margin, and the median is 5.6p (percentage points, "p" from here on). That is a bigger difference than the entire book margin of a company in the 0–3% band.

This is not an illusion created by large profits. A company with a big profit will naturally look low if you divide cash by profit, so the table divides by sales instead. Split the sample into its five industries and all five part only above 12%. The numbers are in the appendix.

Below a 12% margin the books and the bank are the same number. Above it, 100 of book profit brings 72 into the bank.

03But which company changes every year

We opened last year's reports too and re-ran everything on the 307 companies with two years. As a group, they parted above 12% last year just the same. So we followed each company one by one. Of the 59 companies whose two-year average margin exceeds 12%, in how many of the two years was the bank more than 3p thinner than the books?

Under 12%24838%7%

Figure 4 · Four in five of the 12%+ group see it at least once, but only one in three see it two years running

Four in five (81%) of the 12%+ group hit a year in which the bank was thinner than the books. But two years in a row is one in three (31%). Line up each company's gap last year against its gap this year and the correlation is −0.15, meaning there is no rule that a company that parted last year parts again this year. If anything it leans slightly the other way.

Every winter someone in the class catches a cold. Who catches it changes every year. That is the shape here. The parting belongs to the group, and which company gets the year cannot be read from last year's books, because the year of piling up inventory and the year of receivables piling up come to each company in turn.

Profit on paper with cash going out is mostly a one-year event too. Of the companies in that state this year, 37% were in it last year as well. That is more than double the 15% among everyone else, but 63% were fine the year before.

This is the real answer to "high-margin companies have good cash too." As a group, yes. Their 14.6% bank margin is higher than any other band's. But every year it is more than 5p thinner than the books, and a different set of companies produces that gap each year. If you pick companies by margin (lending, trading, investing), four in five of that group will hit a thin-bank year within two years, and last year's numbers will not tell you which.

Four in five of the 12%+ group part at least once, but only one in three two years running. The group parts; the names change every year.

Figure 5 · The more a company advertises, the less of its book profit reaches the bank, and the more often a profitable year ends with cash going out

Book margin is the same in all four bands, around 6%. On the income statement alone, a heavy advertiser and a light one look like the same company. The bank differs. Companies spending more than 10% of sales on advertising receive only 58 of every 100 of book profit, and close to four in ten (36%) saw cash leave the bank in a profitable year.

The reason is dates. Ad money leaves the bank on the day the campaign runs. The sales it creates do not arrive right away. If they went through a marketplace or a distributor, the cash comes on that partner's settlement day, and goods made in advance for the campaign sit in the warehouse until they sell. The income statement has already filled that stretch with "sold."

Figure 6 · Ad money leaves today; the sales it creates arrive on someone else's settlement day

So it gets sharper when we overlay where the company sells. Selling through other people's channels means the audit report shows sales commissions paid.

Without advertising, the channel makes no difference to the bank (15% vs 17%). Advertise heavily and sell through others' channels, and nearly half (44%) of profitable companies see cash leave the bank that year, with only 37 of every 100 of book profit arriving. The same advertising through the company's own channels brings 65.

Ad money leaves today; the sales arrive on someone else's settlement day. The income statement fills the gap with "sold."

05It is not because they are growing fast

The usual explanation here is "they are growing, so cash is tied up in inventory and receivables." It is half right. Companies whose sales grew more than 20% show a books-minus-bank gap of +3.3p, larger than everyone else (near zero). Growth does widen the gap. But whether cash actually leaves the bank has nothing to do with growth: 25% of companies whose sales fell, and 21% of those that grew more than 20%, were profitable with cash going out. Almost the same.

Figure 8 · Fast growth widens the gap between the books and the bank, but the share with cash going out does not move with growth

So "grow fast and the gap widens" is true, and "grow fast and the bank runs dry" is false. They are different sentences. What dries the bank follows advertising and channels, not growth.

06Receivables, or inventory

For the 101 companies with a large gap, we looked at the cash-flow statement to see where the money is sitting. Three in four (73%) lean one way: inventory or receivables.

Figure 9 · The 101 companies with a large gap: inventory 41 · receivables 33 · neither 27

The same "profitable but no cash" is, in one case, goods that did not sell and, in the other, money not yet collected. The remedies are opposites. Inventory is a question of what to make and how much (SKUs and demand forecasts); receivables are a question of which channels to sell through and on what terms. Say only "cash isn't flowing" without knowing which, and you fix neither.

08What these numbers must not be used for

Do not read them as cause and effect. Heavy advertisers may simply be the kind of business that carries inventory (brands, manufacturers), and that may be why they part. We matched on industry, size and growth, but not on "does this business carry inventory." So this piece says only that "heavy advertisers' profit does not become cash," not that "advertising eats cash."

It is only two years. The −0.15 correlation is measured on two years. With three or four, some companies may turn out to have it as a constitution. All we can say now is that with two years, last year does not predict this year.

Advertising counts only the advertising expense line. Companies that book it as sales promotion may be misclassified as light advertisers. That would understate the effect, not overstate it.

No company names. Externally audited companies only, so nothing below a few billion won in sales; fiscal year 2025, mostly separate (non-consolidated) accounts.

09What this piece is saying

The group above a 12% margin receives 72 of every 100 of book profit every year, and a different set of companies produces the gap each year. One income statement shows only that a company is in that group, not who will have the year. To see that, you have to put the income statement, the cash-flow statement and last year's report on the same line for the same company, and then split "which sales became cash" by order, channel and SKU. If post 15 was "which orders came from the ads," this one is "which profit is cash."

Most of our work looks like this. Far more often than data being missing, the data that exists is not in a state that can answer the question. Putting four tables on one line comes before the analysis, and it is usually the genuinely hard part.

Getting data into a state that can answer is what we do.

Appendix — the doubts we tried, and numbers for checking

Four things we tried in order to kill the conclusion. An illusion of large profits? Dividing cash by profit makes high-profit companies look low by construction, so we divided by sales instead. It parts only above 12%. An industry effect? We measured within each of the five industries. In the 12%+ band, books minus bank is health functional foods +6.0 · food +3.7 · beverages +3.1 · e-commerce +3.3 · cosmetics +9.5p; in the band under 5% all five sit between −4.3 and +1.4p. A one-year snapshot? Inventory and receivables are balances on a single closing day and one end-of-year push can move them. Re-measured on last year's reports, the 12%-and-up versus under-12% difference was +5.1p last year (95% interval +3.2 to +6.4). This year it is +6.0p (+3.4 to +8.5). Fast growth? See 05. We also checked the claim that companies piling up inventory are the growing ones: of companies that increased inventory last year, 30% saw sales fall this year, against 57% of those that cut inventory. The ones piling up are indeed the ones growing, and it still has nothing to do with the bank running dry.

Some things disappeared once definitions were fixed. "Companies that cut advertising have the worst cash" vanished when compared at matched margin, ad share and industry. "Margin recovered but profit fell" was an arithmetic error from not excluding last year's loss-makers. Neither is in this piece.

The 32 companies with ads above 10% selling through others' channels have a median book margin of 5.8%, nothing special, and among the 83 companies above 12% this combination is 8 (10%), the same as its share overall (9%). Those 8 show the largest gap, +9.0p, but the other 75 still part by +4.5p. So advertising and channels decide "profitable with cash going out," while the bend at 12% stands without them. Why it bends at 12% is as far as public data goes; that is a place only order- and channel-level books can show.

For checking. In the 12%+ row of table 2, the difference of the two medians (17.5 − 14.6 = 2.8p) is smaller than the median of per-company differences (5.6p) because the companies that part, part widely, rather than many parting a little. A regression (industry, size and growth controlled, 2% trimmed at both ends, n=308) gives 0.5p of bank margin per 1p of book margin (p<0.001), −0.18p per 1p of ad share (p=0.003), and growth is not significant. A logit on "profitable with cash going out" likewise finds ad share significant and growth not. The shares in table 3 sit on rounding boundaries (29.5 · 39.3), so rounding is fixed to half-up.
