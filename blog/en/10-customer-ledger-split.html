The same customer is a different person
in every system Customer data unification · 85 consumer/F&B/hospitality companies, public web records · public-data analysis

Caff · September 2026
Data used: privacy policies, online store structures, notices and corporate filings of 85 companies (measured Aug–Sep 2026). Sample company names are not disclosed. Internal data: our published collaboration figures only.

When I took over a retail data hub where the orders of 1.95 million customers and 256 brands came together, the first thing waiting for me wasn't some sophisticated machine-learning model. It was the records. The same product was scattered across half a dozen differently spelled names, and the same customer carried a different member ID on every channel. Nobody could answer a question as basic as "how many regulars do we have?" The trouble wasn't a missing number. There were several. Analysis could only begin once those records were stitched back into one person, one product.

01What happened

Was that just one odd company? No. All 96 brands that passed through the hub were in the same shape. Gentle Monster, RAWROW, Antoine Solais, Hunter: big or small, thriving or not, their records at the starting line looked the same. Half the daily work at the hub wasn't modeling at all. It was two matching jobs: finding the same person, and finding the same product.

So what about companies outside the hub? This time, we counted.

02It wasn't just my hub: 84 out of 85

You can't see inside a company from the street. But you can read what a company has written down about itself: its privacy policy (which by law must list the systems and processors that handle customer data), the structure of its online stores, official notices, corporate registrations and filings. The sample: 85 consumer, F&B and hospitality companies, measured August–September 2026.

The counting is simple. For each company, we counted the paths along which one customer's records can split apart:

  • two or more own online stores?
  • two or more customer-management systems?
  • multiple sales/booking channels?
  • separate legal entities per brand?
  • M&A history?

Fragmentation rate (%) = (companies with ≥1 path) ÷ 85 × 100. The result: 84 of 85 companies, or 99%. Inside the hub or out, almost everyone.

By customer-system count, 45 companies (53%) run two or more, and the maximum is 17, at one hospitality group, listed among the processors in its own privacy policy. When bookings arrive through six channels and customer records sit in seventeen systems, even "is this guest a repeat visitor?" has no answer.

We counted only what each company had written down about itself. Even that was enough to find fragmentation at 84 of 85.

A caveat up front: these figures are a strict floor. We counted only what is publicly written, so fragmentation that never reaches a policy page (a manager's spreadsheet, a dealer's paper roster, the legacy database a departed employee built) is not included. The real figure is worse.

03Why "just buy a CDP" is wrong

If one system fixed this, this piece would not exist. The measurement says otherwise: fragmentation comes in layers. Count the paths per company: only one company in 85 has none, the median is two layers, and two in five (36 companies, 42%) carry three or more at once.

Figure 2 · Fragmentation layers per company (same 85 companies). One company has zero; two in five have three or more

One system can't fix it because each layer needs a different remedy. Multiple online stores are an ID-mapping problem. Separate legal entities are a consent-and-contract problem, which makes them a legal one. Acquired brands are an excavation problem: finding the customer database left behind in someone else's system. Buying a system solves only the first layer. Leave the rest in place and you get the familiar complaint: "we integrated, but the numbers don't match."

Buying a system takes care of the first layer. Every layer after that needs a decision before it needs a budget.

For the same reason, "our own team can just pull it" is only half right. For the first layer it is true: with someone who writes good SQL, mapping IDs across stores takes days. But SQL won't get you through the legal-entity layer. That one is a question of whether you have grounds to bring over another entity's customer data, and no amount of query skill creates grounds that aren't there. Acquisitions are the same: when nobody knows which system still holds the data, adding people to pull it changes nothing. Some layers are solved by adding people; others need a decision before anyone is added. Start without telling them apart and you end up saying "we've been integrating for six months."

One more distinction matters. There are two kinds of fragmentation. The four paths above are data you could join but haven't. Platform and marketplace sales are a different kind. As the supplements piece put it, sell on your own store and the buyer's ID, repeat cycle and drop-offs all land on your own server; sell on a marketplace and what you receive is a settlement statement. You don't learn who that customer is later. You never learn it. The larger your platform share, the more your own channels become the only window onto your customers, and the measurement above says that even that window is fragmented at 99% of companies. You can't fix data you never receive, which makes joining the data you do receive all the more urgent.

04What fragmented records hide

The numbers still add up. The questions just stop being answerable.

Repeat rate breaks. Someone who buys on your own store and again on your marketplace storefront counts as two new customers when the records are split. Repeat rate reads too low and new-customer inflow too high, and the marketing budget follows those wrong numbers into "acquisition."

Segmentation breaks. RFM's F comes down to deciding "what counts as one purchase." With purchases scattered across three systems, there is nothing coherent to count. A loyal customer shows up as three one-time shoppers.

Incrementality breaks. Whether an ad and a promotion reached the same customer is visible only once orders are joined per person; per-channel records will never show it.

Churn is a definition, not an event. This matters most for memberships and subscriptions. Most teams pull their churn list by expiry date, but an expiry date is a calendar entry, not a signal. People don't leave because the term ended; the term ends after they have already left. The real signal comes earlier, in behavior: visits spacing out, same-day cancellations rising, the usual weekday shifting. And that behavior is scattered across the app, the on-site POS and the consultation notes. If a member who books in the app and pays at the counter exists as two people in two systems, "time between visits" can't be computed at all. It is RFM's F all over again: deciding what counts as churn comes before any model.

And people aren't the only thing that splits. Products split too. When the same SKU is registered under channel-specific names, options and bundles, you can count neither its repeat purchases nor its margin by channel.

Finally, a newer casualty: there is no history for AI to work from. To make an AI persona act as your customer, you have to hand it a line like "over the past year you bought this brand three times, most recently on discount." Aggregate rates won't get it talking; a later piece in this series measures exactly that. The catch is that this line can only be written from a unified history. With split records, there is no such line to write.

Split records don't give you wrong numbers. They take away the questions: repeat rate, segments, incrementality, and the one line a persona needs.

05What happens when you join them

We have done this. Together with Gentle Monster, we joined orders that had been arriving separately by channel into per-customer histories and built RFM segments on top. The result was simple: click rates and revenue lined up in exactly the order the segments were cut. The model wasn't especially clever. What changed was that "what counts as one purchase" could finally be decided on unified records. A CRM campaign run on the same foundation lifted own-store revenue 4.3× in six weeks (ROAS 5,370%, verified by z-test). The campaign wasn't brilliant either; for the first time, we could see who not to send to.

The order matters. Unification comes before analysis. On split records, the more sophisticated the model, the more precisely wrong it is.

06So what should your company do?

Two things: one check today, one experiment on Monday.

Today: open your company's privacy policy. It is the same method this piece used. If the list of processors and systems runs past five lines, that list is a map of how many pieces your customer data is in. No meeting needed; it takes five minutes.

Monday: re-measure your repeat rate, once. Pull the last twelve months of orders from your top two channels and join them on a single identifier; a phone number is enough. This isn't a development project, it's a one-day job. The gap between that number and the repeat rate on your dashboard is the size of what fragmentation has been hiding from you. If the gap is small, relax and move on. If it's large, that gap is your ROI estimate for a unification project. Either way, you have the answer in a day.

Then find your company's row. Each layer has a different first move.

07What this piece is really saying

This work usually gets filed as boring cleanup before the analysis. But if 84 of 85 companies are fragmented, joining is the scarcest work there is. Anyone can build a dashboard, and everyone uses the same models. Confirming that the customer in three systems is one person means re-architecting the data, and once that is done, repeat rates, segments, incrementality and AI personas all start working at once. That is what we sell.
