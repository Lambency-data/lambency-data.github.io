The registry was open.
The efficacy field was 65% empty Two public MFDS functional-cosmetics registries · 216,491 rows · why efficacy sits in three fields and two lists

Caff · November 2026
Data used — full public-data-portal snapshots of the MFDS functional-cosmetics reported and reviewed lists (September 21, 2026) and the 198 administrative actions published on the MFDS portal (October 15, 2026). Zero internal data.

"Whitening", "anti-wrinkle", "UV protection". You see these words on every product page. To use them, the product has to be a functional cosmetic, a Korean regulatory category for products that make claims like these, and it has to be filed with the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety (MFDS) for that function, and the report is open to anyone on Korea's public data portal. I wanted to ask "is this product really reported for whitening?" in one line of code. So I downloaded the whole registry. 195,799 rows in the reported list, 20,692 in the reviewed list (the two filing routes are explained below), 216,491 rows in all. Downloading took less than half a day. Making it usable took a good while longer. Between "open" and "usable" there were three things. Efficacy is not in one field, there are two lists, and a product name does not point to a product. This piece measures each of the three, and at the end I release the tool that does it in a single query.

01Efficacy is not in one field

At first I read only the efficacy document field. The name said so, so I assumed it was there. 65% of it was empty. A reported list with no efficacy? I opened the other fields, and efficacy was scattered across three. Fill rate (rows with a value ÷ all rows) comes to 59.9% for the efficacy name field, 34.6% for the efficacy document field, 21.1% for the sun protection factor (SPF) field. Read all three together and 94.7% of rows are filled; 5.3% are empty in all three. Rows with an efficacy name but an empty document field alone number 117,327.

A lookup that reads one field returns "no efficacy data" for six in ten products that were properly reported. Trust that answer and you can no longer tell "not reported" from "I read the wrong field." This is usually what makes data unusable. The values are right; they just sit in a field you aren't reading.

02There are two lists

A functional cosmetic enters through one of two routes: "report" or "review". Make it with ingredients and amounts already on the official list and a report is enough; anything else goes to review. On the public data portal the two are separate services you apply for separately, and the field names differ. The reported list has 195,799 rows, the reviewed list 20,692, and only 123 names appear in both. The reviewed list has its efficacy document field filled in 90.8% of rows. So someone who looks at the reviewed list first comes to believe the document field is never empty, and that belief breaks the moment they move to the reported list.

Figure 2 · Size of the two lists and how often the efficacy document field is filled. 123 names appear in both

Query one list and a product on the other comes back "not found". "Not found" is not the same as "not reported", but on screen they look identical.

03A product name does not point to a product

The third one took longest. I assumed searching by product name would do. In the reported list, after removing spaces, 2,930 names have two or more reports, covering 6,214 rows. For 361 of those names, different companies reported under the same name. Among names reported more than once, the share split between different companies (361 ÷ 2,930) is 12.3%. When a name repeats, about one time in eight someone else's product is in the pile. The same company filing the same name several times has its reasons: a different size, a different shade, a reformulation filed again. But 361 names shared by different companies aren't variants; they're a trap. Search by name, take the first row as "the product", and you end up judging your page against someone else's report.

Figure 3 · 2,930 names reported more than once, 6,214 rows, and 361 names shared by different companies

So the name finds candidates and the report number makes the call. A name is not a key. Nor is this a registry quirk. Customer names in a company's records and product names in its order data are the same trap.

Between open data and usable data there were three fields, two lists, and "a name is not a key".

04One query: bytemescan

I released a lookup that handles all three as open source (MIT). It is called bytemescan. It queries both lists at once, reads the three efficacy fields together, and when given only a product name it does not decide; it returns candidates with their report numbers. Ask again by number and you get "matches the report" or "does not." That's why the 2,930 names in §03 matter. In an AI coding tool such as Claude Code, one line of setup lets you ask "is this product reported for this function?" directly. Installation, the verdict table and the order of use are on the tool page and in the repository.

The number of candidates a name returns decides the next step. One rule is absolute: if a name returns candidates from more than one company, no verdict is given without a report number.

05What this tool does not do

It is not a legal verdict. "Does not match" means the page differs from what the public registry says was reported; the judgment after that is a person's.

Most advertising penalties happen outside the registry. Of the 198 cosmetics administrative actions published on the MFDS portal (actions between October 2025 and October 2026, collected October 15, 2026), 137 were about advertising. The largest group, 81 cases (59%), was wording that could be mistaken for a medicine. Wording that looks like a functional claim on a product that is not a functional cosmetic was 7 cases (5%). Registry matching speaks directly to the 7. The 81 turn on whether a sentence reads like a drug, a judgment the registry does not contain. Of the 137 advertising cases, 135 were an advertising suspension for the product in question, most often for 3 months.

Figure 4 · Of 198 published actions, 137 concerned advertising; 81 were drug-like wording and 7 functional-like wording. The registry speaks to the 7

It does not identify which product a sales page is selling. Sales pages rarely carry a report number. Grouping the same product across several sellers, reading the text inside detail images, and comparing document to document against the registry are outside this open tool.

06So what now

Public data is free; making it usable is not. For this registry, the work was merging three fields, joining two lists, and judging by number instead of name. Data inside a company is no different. The ad report, the order database and the CRM each hold the same customer in a different field under a different name, and joining them comes before any analysis. For the registry, that first step is now open to anyone.

What comes after (grouping pages scattered across sellers into one reported product, reading the wording inside images, and comparing it against the registry) is what the byteme sales-page check does. The case-by-case look at the 198 actions and the sales-page checklist are on the byteme blog (in Korean).
