What score gets you a Seoul apartment?
Every cut-off of the last 12 months Housing-subscription scores · 28 Seoul private-APT notices · 214 units, exhaustive · public-data analysis

Caff · September 2026
Data used — three Cheongyak Home open APIs (sale notices, competition ratios, winner scores; data.go.kr). Sep 2025–Aug 2026: 28 Seoul private-apartment notices, 27 with published cut-offs, all 214 units. Zero internal data.

Right after "maybe I should apply" comes the same question every time — "so what score do I need?" We collected every published minimum winning score from Cheongyak Home and answered it with numbers. Over the last twelve months, the median winning cut-off for Seoul private-apartment general supply was 64 points. Even at 60 points, barely one in five units (22%) had its door open; at 50 points, four in a hundred (4%). But once the data was in, the real finding was elsewhere. 64 is not a coincidence. We'll get to that in a moment.

01What we measured

We collected every published minimum winning score from Cheongyak Home (Korea Real Estate Board). From September 2025 through August 2026: 28 Seoul private-apartment notices — 27 of them with published cut-offs, split into 214 units by housing type — general supply, local (Seoul-resident) quota.

The arithmetic is simple: was my score at or above that unit's minimum winning score? We call that share "units with an open door" — coverage (%) = (units where my score ≥ minimum winning score) ÷ 214 × 100.

One line to draw before anything else: this is a comparison against past cut-offs, not a probability of winning. The same score splits differently depending on ties and supply, and next year's distribution will not equal last year's. Still, knowing where your score sits in the distribution — versus not knowing — is a large difference.

02The notches in the cut-offs are family sizes

The distribution piles up at 64–69. Why there? The answer was in the scoring formula, not the data. The score is a sum of three items: years without owning a home (max 32), dependents (5 points for yourself, 5 more per person), and years on the subscription account (max 17). Suppose you max out the first and the third. From that moment, the only variable left is how many of you there are.

The median cut-off, 64, is exactly the perfect score of a three-person household. A family of three that maxes out — fifteen years without owning, fifteen years on the account — gets a perfect score and finds exactly half of Seoul's doors open. A couple's perfect 59 opens one in five (21%); a solo applicant's perfect 54, one in ten (10%). Even a family of four's 69 left 15% closed, and one complex in Seocho (Acro de Seocho) cleared only at 84 — a perfect score.

So this distribution does not say "Seoul is full of high scorers." The cut-off distribution is not the shape of demand — it is the shadow of the ceilings the system builds over each household size. Time fills in the other two items; dependents it does not. Which is why the real answer to "what score do I need" is less a number than "how many of you are there?"

Figure 2 · Distribution of winning cut-offs — the same 214 units, 5-point bins. Dashed line: median 64

We also counted where the 15% that even a perfect 69 could not open actually is. Of the 32 units with cut-offs at 70 or above, 18 are in Seocho-gu. More than half of "the Seoul where even maxed-out families lose" is really the story of a single district.

03Where the dammed-up demand goes — leftovers at 276:1

This is the coldest number in the piece. The standard advice for anyone under the ceiling is "try the post-sale leftovers" — homes whose contracts fell through, resold by straight draw with no points at all. So we counted every leftover sale in the same Seoul, over the same twelve months: 999 homes supplied, 276,141 applications — 276:1 overall, a median of 42:1 per unit, and at the top, one home in Yeongdeungpo drew 130,938 applicants.

This number and the table above are one body. When the points system builds a ceiling over each household size, the demand under it does not disappear — it pours into the doors that ignore points. The leftover round became a lottery not by accident but because it is the points system's overflow drain. "Try the leftovers" is not wrong; at 276:1 it is just not a strategy. It is buying a ticket.

04The doors that actually open are types, not luck

The lowest cut-offs: 29 points (De Pine Artia 110㎡A), 32 points (The Richmond Mia 49㎡). What they share is very large or very small — housing types demand passed by. Those doors did not open because low scorers won; they were open because nobody was queuing. "Complexes that opened at low scores" were a matter of type selection.

05So what do I actually do

Your score is not something you can change. The one thing you choose is which line to stand in. Read your household's row — every number is the same measurement as above.

06Why these numbers were scattered

Every number in this piece is public. The reason you have never seen this picture is that the cut-offs are scattered across notice pages, mixed with unpublished "-" entries, and split by housing type and residency — no human hand turns them into one picture. You have to stitch together all 214 units, plus 114 leftover units, before these tables exist. We are the team that prices non-apartment property — multi-family homes, shops, small buildings — from actual transactions, and we ran this measurement so that next to "neighbourhoods you can reach with ₩300M" the same screen can say how far open the alternative door of a new-build subscription actually was.
