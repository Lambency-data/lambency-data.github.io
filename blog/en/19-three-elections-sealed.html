Three Elections, Sealed Before the Vote
We Did Not Beat the Polling Average Brazil's runoff · Israel's general election · the US midterms · forecasts locked before the vote, scored as they were after the count

Caff · November 2026
Data used — three forecast files sealed before the vote and the official counts (Brazil TSE, Israel Central Elections Committee, US en.wikipedia tallies, final scoring November 12). Polls from public aggregation tables. Zero internal data.

On the night of November 1, two days before the US midterms, I was running a last check on the locked forecast file and found a missing item in the Nebraska calculation. It was the item for the candidate's personal strength. Adding it moved the number by more than 2 points, and the arithmetic showed right there that the move would be toward the right answer. I did not add it. The file had gone to a public repository with its fingerprint (hash) the day before, and changing a number there would have made the previous two months of work meaningless. Two days later, Nebraska was wrong.

Over the past two months I forecast three elections this way. Write the forecast into a file before the vote, leave the file's fingerprint so it cannot be changed later, and score that file as it is once the count is in. Brazil's presidential runoff on October 25, Israel's general election on October 27, the US midterms on November 3. The results first. In the US we were off by an average of 3.47 points on the vote share in five Senate states, while a forecast that simply used the polling average was off by 2.94. We did worse. In Brazil we called the winner in 26 of 27 states and got the national winner wrong. In Israel we were off by an average of 4.0 seats across three blocs, and only the Arab parties' seats fell outside the range we had written down in advance. Set side by side, the three looked the same. The polls under-counted one side, and our forecasts, built on the polls, were wrong with them. This piece is the record of those three, and of what we learned from the misses to take into Korea's 2028 general election.

01What locking before the vote means

The method is simple. The forecast vote share for each state and the rules that produced it go into one file. The file's SHA-256 hash, in plain words its fingerprint, is published before the vote. The fingerprint changes if even one character of the file changes, so anyone can check after the count whether the numbers were touched. Once the count is in, that file is scored as it is.

One more thing is locked alongside it: a comparison. A forecast that uses the polling average as it is, or last election's result as it is, something anyone can build in ten minutes, is locked at the same moment. It is there to show whether our forecast is any better. If it is worse, we write that it was worse. That is where the title of this piece comes from.

There were three times I wanted to change something. The first was September 2, right after the first US forecast went up. In Maine and Nebraska, two ways of calculating gave answers more than 11 points apart. One used coefficients measured from past counts; the other used what the AI knew about the candidates. My first thought was that one of the two was clearly off and should be fixed. But which one was right could not be known before the count on November 3. Fix it without evidence and it stops being a forecast and becomes tidying. We left the split as it was and decided to learn from the scoring. The other two times come below.

From the US onward, the fingerprints did not stay only in our repository. On the morning of November 1 the forecast file, the rules and the scoring code went to the OSF (Open Science Framework), a pre-registration site for research. The registration is tup6z; the seven additional states locked the same day are x85tm; the House national vote on November 3 is ghuj6. All three are public and carry a DOI. A timestamp in our own repository is a record we made; a registration time at OSF is a record someone else made, which is worth more. For Brazil and Israel we were not ready in time, so only the fingerprints exist.

03Brazil — three weeks of watching the polls move the other way

In early October, right after the first round, we locked forecasts for 27 states. What we forecast was the share the right-wing candidate, Flávio Bolsonaro, would get between the two finalists. Nationally it was 50.19%, a very narrow edge for the right. We locked one more forecast alongside it. In the first round the polls had put the right-wing candidate 1.9 points below his actual result, so this version assumed the runoff polls would do the same and added that much, 52.09% nationally. Then for three weeks we watched the runoff polls come in. They moved the opposite way from our forecast. The 35 polls of the final week averaged 51.1% for the right-wing candidate, and the count came in at 48.21%. This time the polls had put the right 2.89 points too high. Polls that ran low in the first round ran high in the runoff. The direction flipped.

What happened in those three weeks? In the first round on October 4, Flávio Bolsonaro finished nearly 2 points ahead of Lula, and the first televised debate was held on October 18. Local commentary said Flávio dodged questions and spoke thinly in that debate. The second debate on October 23 drew split reviews. The weekly poll average went from 52.1% to 50.9% in the debate week and then stopped at 51.1% in the final week. The debate did move the polls by about a point. The count then went another 2.9 points. It would be simple to write that the debate decided it, but our data does not let us. The movement the polls caught was small; the movement they missed was large.

Where the missed votes came from shows up when you add up the count. The 9.32 million votes that went to candidates eliminated in the first round, plus 4.78 million new votes in the runoff, make 14.10 million. Those votes were split between the two finalists, and 10.38 million of them, 74%, went to Lula's side. The eliminated candidates were mostly classed as centrist or right-wing. Votes that had gone to right-wing candidates did not go right in the runoff. The polls trusted that classification, and so did we. This is a sum of the count, so it does not prove where any one person moved. It does say which side the 2.9-point gap came from.

The map was right. State mean error 1.83 points, winners called in 26 of 27 states, and with the national average removed, the relative ordering of the states was off by only 0.36 points. The one thing wrong was the national average, and that one thing got the national winner wrong. The forecast that added the last polling error was worse: an error of 3.57 points, double. Adding last time's error to this time failed here. But dropping it straight away would also be changing the rule after seeing the result. That thought carried into Israel two days later.

04Israel — the morning after a failure, locking the same method again

On the morning of October 26, the day after Brazil's runoff, we locked Israel's general election. What we forecast was the number of seats for three blocs: the coalition camp, the Arab parties, and the rest of the opposition. The main forecast used the polls from the last ten days as they were: coalition camp 52 seats, Arab parties 14, the rest 54. And we locked one test forecast as well. Over the last five elections the polls had missed the same blocs in the same direction, so this version added that much. Twelve hours earlier we had watched exactly that method double the error in Brazil. I wanted to pull it and did not. Changing the rule on the strength of one result is also changing it after seeing the result. Instead, the locking document said this: this forecast is a test, and the score is taken from the main forecast.

The count: coalition camp 53 seats, Arab parties 19, the rest 48. The main forecast's mean error was 4.0 seats; the comparison that used the 2022 result as it was came to 7.33 seats. We had the coalition short of the 61-seat majority, and that was right. The probability of a majority we had written down when locking was 0.056. Two of the three blocs landed inside the 80% range we had written down in advance; only the Arab parties fell outside. The 80% range is the interval we write down beforehand as the one the actual value should land in eight times out of ten. The polls under-counted the Arab parties by 5 seats, and we followed the polls.

Why were 5 seats missed? Turnout. By the Central Elections Committee's count, national turnout was 77.7%, the highest since 1999. Turnout among Arab voters rose from about 53% in 2022 to about 77% this time. Pre-election polls had been assuming around 65%. When we split the gain in Arab seats, 3.4 seats came from the Arab parties merging their lists so that no votes were wasted, and 5.5 seats came from the votes themselves increasing. The polls missed the second part. All 24 polls from 8 pollsters in the ten days before locking had the Arab parties at 13 to 16 seats. It was not an error you could avoid by choosing the right pollster. Reports gave several reasons for the jump in turnout, and our data cannot tell how many seats each reason was worth. One thing can be written down. A turnout jump of more than 20 points in one group did not exist in the errors of the last five elections, so it did not exist in the range we wrote down either.

The polls missed one more thing in the same election: Likud. Our forecast had it at 22 seats; the actual was 27. For a single party that is a large error. Grouped into the coalition camp, the error shrank to 1 seat, because within the same bloc one party rose while others fell. Locking at bloc level was lucky this time. I do not claim it will be next time.

Figure 3 · Forecast and actual seats for Israel's three blocs. Only the Arab parties are outside the range written down in advance

The test forecast's error was 2.67 seats. Better than the main forecast. This was the third time I wanted to change something. The correction worked in Israel, so why not record 2.67 seats as the score: that thought comes immediately. We do not record it that way. Renaming a forecast we had written down as a test into the main forecast after seeing the result is exactly changing things after the result. Our score is 4.0 seats. The fact that the test forecast did better is recorded separately, and what it means is read in 06, next to Brazil.

In Brazil, adding the last polling error doubled the error. In Israel, the same method cut it by a third. Only with the two side by side can you see when it works and when it does not.

05The US — we did not beat the polling average

For the US we locked the share the Democratic candidate would get between the two candidates in five Senate states on October 31, three days before the vote, and registered it at OSF the next day. Scored: mean error 3.47 points, winners 4/5 across the five states. The polling average locked at the same moment: error 2.94 points, winners 5/5. We did not beat it. In the seven additional states, six are scored so far, and there it was nearly even, 2.35 points for us against 2.42 for the polling average. A comparison forecast whose rules were set on September 1 and never changed scored 3.9 points, inside the pass line of 4 points we had set in advance, but that too was worse than the polling average.

Figure 4 · Errors in the five US states. Negative means the Democratic share was put below the actual. Four of the five point the same way

The state we got wrong is Nebraska, the one from the opening. We put the Democratic share 3.76 points too high. With the missing item it would have been right; without it, it was wrong. This should not read as a complaint. Without the rule that a mistake found before the election still cannot change the locked file, nothing stops anyone from saying after the election that they actually knew. Nebraska is the price of keeping that rule.

The direction matters more than the size of the error. In 10 of the 11 states scored so far, we put the Democratic side below the actual. That is not a different mistake in each state; it looks like one common cause underneath, and the polling average missed in the same direction. The House national vote tells the same story. We put the Democratic share of the two-party vote at 53.54%; the count so far says 56.9%. The probability side held up. In every state where we gave the favourite a 0.80 or higher chance, that candidate won, and the 80% range contained the actual value in four of the five states.

We were not alone. Pew Research's first look after the election found that the polls got the direction right but understated the size of the Democratic advantage. One tally counted 216 Senate and governor polls tilted an average of 4.1 points toward Republicans (Pushbuild); another found Democrats under-counted in 14 of 15 close Senate races (AllSides). Three reasons are being discussed: the models that guess who will vote were built on the electorate of the 2024 presidential election, conservative respondents were given too much weight, and the attempt to correct the misses of the last two elections went too far the other way. All of these are hypotheses until the final counts are in. Our own forecast carries one assumption of the same kind. We weighted turnout by age on the pattern of the 2022 midterms, when turnout was 25.6% among 18 to 24 year olds and 64.6% among those 65 and over. If younger voters turned out above that this time, part of our error came from there. We check when the turnout statistics are published.

One thing to state plainly. This forecast was not made by asking AI characters (LLM personas). A diagnostic forecast run in September with AI characters alone had an error of 8.2 points, and under the rules set at locking it is published as a failure. Since then, election forecasts are made by a calculation model built from coefficients measured in past elections and from polls, and the AI characters are used only for diagnosis, to look at why something went wrong.

06What you only see with the three together

First, adding the last polling error to this forecast works only when the conditions are right. Brazil added the first-round error of the same election to the runoff, the direction flipped, and it failed. Israel added an error that had pointed the same way in four of the last five elections, and it worked. Add it only when several elections have missed in the same direction, and never within the same election. We can write this sentence because the test forecast was not pulled the morning after Brazil. Pulled, the conclusion would have been one line, that corrections do not work, and it would have been wrong. Three countries do not make a law. Only the shape of the condition is written down.

Second, the biggest error is the whole polling industry missing together. All 35 polls of Brazil's final runoff week missed to the same side, and in the US 10 of the 11 states missed in the same direction. This is not the kind of thing you can fix state by state. The only answer is to lock not a single number but a range that separately allows for everyone being wrong together. That is why the 80% range in the US held four of the five states.

Third, a simple comparison is hard to beat. In the US the polling average was better than our forecast. That is why the comparison is locked alongside, and any calculation that does not beat it is dropped from the next forecast. The easiest way to look like a winner is not to lock the comparison.

Lock in advance, score against the comparison, and write it down when you lose. What this method produces is not the forecast but that discipline.

07What we take into Korea's 2028 general election

These three were practice. The real test is the 23rd National Assembly election on April 12, 2028. We do not transplant the three countries' values into Korea. We take only what to look at and which way things tend to go wrong, re-measure on past Korean elections, and use it only where the error goes down. What we have decided to take so far:

After writing the table I asked one more thing. For the three places we were wrong, did data exist before the vote? It did. Where the votes of Brazil's eliminated candidates would go was in the polls' cross-tables, not the poll averages. Brazilian pollsters publish, in every survey, a table crossing whom people voted for in the first round with whom they will vote for in the runoff. We used the transfer rates of the 2022 runoff instead. Israel's Arab turnout had been tracked for weeks by surveys that ask Arab society specifically, not by national polls, and the seats saved by merging the lists were arithmetic that could be done before locking. The US direction was outside the polls: how much one side had outperformed its usual level in the 2025 special elections, and which way the party registration of returned early ballots leaned before election day. Both existed three days before the vote.

So from now on one more forecast is locked each time. A forecast that splits the eliminated candidates' votes by cross-table, one that adds surveys of turnout by group, one that adds the direction of special elections and early votes. All as tests, locked at the same moment as the main forecast, with the same standing as the comparison. If one beats the main forecast it becomes the main forecast next time; if not, it is dropped. It is written down plainly that these data were chosen after watching three elections. Picking, after the result, the data that would have made us right is exactly what this piece warns against, and there is only one way to clear that suspicion: lock before the next election and be scored. In Korea the most useful of the three is early-voting turnout. Early voting runs for two days starting five days before election day, and turnout by region is published during those two days. In 2028 we lock both a forecast three days before the vote and one the day before that uses the early-voting turnout. The day before falls inside the period when publishing polls is banned, so no numbers are released; only the fingerprint is kept, and it is opened after the count.

08The scoring date, and what this piece does not do

The US numbers were scored for the final time on November 12, 2026. Compared with the provisional scoring of November 8, the numbers moved a little and the conclusions did not. Of the seven additional states, Alaska is still waiting for an official winner, so six were scored. The House national vote will be corrected in one line when the count closes on December 15. Brazil and Israel are final.

This piece does not say which candidate or party is favoured. It records only where our method went wrong. It also does not say that AI called the election. The forecasts were made by a calculation model built from coefficients measured in past elections and from polls; the AI characters were used for diagnosis. Taiwan's local elections were locked as well, but no numbers appear until the local ban on publication ends.

Back to the night of November 1: should the item have gone in? With it, Nebraska would have been right and the winners 5/5. The answer is still the same. A number made that way would have been one nobody could trust. What this method produces is not the forecast itself. It is the discipline of locking in advance, scoring against the comparison, and writing it down when you lose. The price of keeping that discipline is, now and then, a Nebraska. Elections are the most transparent test there is, because the answer is published, so we try it here first. The same discipline goes into CRM message tests and product-response forecasts.
