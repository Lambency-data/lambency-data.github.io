DeepCaffein · Public Data Series · 06 · Pharmaceuticals

The data is public.
Nobody's using it Pharmaceuticals · HIRA prescription data × drug registry · dose mix and regional demand · public-data analysis

Caff · August 2026
Data used: the drug-utilization open API of HIRA, Korea's Health Insurance Review and Assessment Service (top 100 classes × 3 time points; district and ingredient level; 3,695 queries), the list mapping reimbursed drugs to ATC codes (the WHO drug classification), the resident population registry, HIRA research reports and ministry releases, peer-reviewed literature. Zero internal data.

I spent the first months of the pandemic on a hospital data team in the United States. There was no vaccine yet, and the doctors were testing drugs that might work. My job was to make those tests statistically sound, starting with the math of how many patients you need before a difference stops being luck. Here is what stayed with me. The same drug looked different at different doses, and sometimes the difference dissolved when you measured it properly. But one thing was unambiguous: where dose reliably made a difference was safety, not efficacy. The high-dose arm got into trouble first. The lesson I took from those months is simple: with the same molecule, a different dose is a different drug.

01The note-parsing years

This isn't a story about making vaccines. I'm a data person, and that work led me to a healthcare IT company that collected oncology data from American hospitals. The data lived in three systems: Epic and Cerner, which run whole hospitals, and iKnowMed, used by community oncology clinics. They store the same items, but every hospital recorded them differently: some as codes, some in other units, some not at all. Every new hospital meant redoing the mapping from scratch.

The data had to flow in real time. If the pipeline died overnight, the next morning's clinic saw yesterday's numbers, so we rotated on-call shifts through weekends and holidays. And the hardest part was the prescriptions themselves. What drug a patient received, at how many milligrams, was in no column at all. It was inside the sentences of the physician's notes. Building the rules that pulled drug names and doses out of those sentences was half the project. The notes also carried patient identifiers, and in the end the project was stopped over exactly that. That was prescription data as I knew it: something you build by hand until you're forced to stop.

02In Korea, it's a table

That is why I paused when I first opened Korea's public health-insurance data. Here, that data is a table. Ingredient, dose, and formulation live inside a single nine-digit code, and prescription quantities and amounts for each code are published by district, by month, nationwide, back to 2010. No notes to parse, and no privacy problem, because it is aggregated. It sits there, tidier than what we built on weekend shifts, for anyone to look at.

But public and usable turned out to be different things. The data sits behind a query screen, with no download. Pick one therapeutic class, one region and one month, and you get one table. The interface is built for a person clicking with a mouse: assembling the national picture in this piece took 3,695 queries over two days, at whatever pace the server would tolerate. The official bulk files don't offer this combination at all. The data is open; access is narrow. In the US the obstacle was the physician's note. Here it is access.

You can count how many people have actually used it. On Korea's open-data portal, applications for this prescription-data API total 2,352 in ten years (as of 2026-08-23). The hospital-location data the same agency opened the same year has 8,606. Apartment transaction prices drew 16,874 applications within a year and a half of being listed. On GitHub, public repositories that use this API: zero. Repositories that use real-estate price data: 248. Property data drew more than sixteen thousand applications in a year and a half. This API drew a little over two thousand in a decade, and we could not find a single public analysis to show for them. Why? Our guess is that three things are at work: house prices are everyone's business, while prescription data concerns a single industry; that industry is used to buying estimates rather than building them; and access is narrow.

So the gap in this industry looks different from the ones earlier in the series. Until now the gaps were data that didn't exist (the food registry records no deaths) or data someone else controls (the shelf data belongs to the retailer). Here it is the opposite: the data is wide open, and nobody is using it. So let's use it.

This piece follows one drug: the lipid-lowering combination, a statin and ezetimibe in a single pill, sold under names like Rosuzet, a leading Korean brand. Why this one? Over the past five years it became Korea's most-prescribed drug class. You would expect the best-selling drug to be the one designed with the best knowledge of demand. The data shows something closer to the opposite. Here is the story, in order.

03A map of the market: its shape is changing

Data first. We took the 100 therapeutic classes with the most reimbursed products and pulled nationwide claims for three months: June 2019, June 2024, and March 2026, the latest month available. Monthly reimbursement for these 100 classes went from ₩720 billion to ₩1.19 trillion in five years. That is 64% growth, but what stands out more than the growth is that the market is changing shape.

As of 2024, the biggest class isn't statins but the lipid combination. At ₩78 billion a month, 4.4 times its level five years earlier, it has overtaken single-ingredient statins (₩62.6 billion, +50%). The same pattern shows up elsewhere: blood-pressure combinations are up 94% and 279%, and diabetes combinations 118%. Much of the combinations' rise is substitution: lipid drugs as a whole grew 135% in five years, while the combination share within them went from 35% to 55%. A market of one ingredient per pill has crossed the halfway mark toward a market of several.

Other classes tell their own stories on the same map. In acid suppression, the older generation (H2 blockers) fell 34%, the only decline in the top 100, while the newer generation (PPIs) grew 174% to fill the space. The class containing choline alfoscerate, a "brain-function" drug whose reimbursement is under review over disputed efficacy, still moves ₩36.4 billion a month. But this piece doesn't linger there; it follows the class that became number one.

The data also shows the order in which the combination spread. Divide each region's prescriptions by its population (1.0 means proportional). In 2019, when the market was a third of its current size, Seoul was highest at 1.31: the new drug caught on in Seoul first. The five years since brought growth everywhere, with Seoul's per-capita prescriptions up 3.3-fold. The rest of the country simply grew faster: Gwangju and Daegu 4-fold, Jeju 4.3-fold, Sejong 5.2-fold. By 2024, per-capita prescriptions in Gwangju and Daegu (2.6 tablets per person per month) had reached Seoul's level. Seoul did not fall back; the rest of the country caught up. In the chart below, the multiples converging toward 1.0 show it. Who learned from whom is not visible in this data. What is visible is who adopted first, who followed, and how fast the gap closed.

04Same drug: which doses sell

This is the part this piece was written for. Once combinations pass half the market, the number of products to make multiplies. Two ingredients make a pair, and every pair splits again by dose. What to make, and how much, becomes a far harder question.

Start with supply. The 21,953 reimbursed drugs collapse to 2,268 ingredients by code. The key point is that the nine-digit code holds more than the ingredient: four digits for the molecule, two for the dose, three for the form. The dose already has its own field in the code. The exact distinction we tried to parse out of physicians' notes in the US is a single field here. Two-thirds of ingredients (65.5%) come in a single dose, but the other end of the range is crowded. Donepezil, the dementia drug, comes in ten dose-forms, made by 122 companies as 288 products. Rosuvastatin comes in seven, from 124 companies, as 330 products. For hospitals and pharmacies this is an inventory problem: stocking one drug means choosing among hundreds of products.

And because the dose is in the code, demand can be read by dose as well. We broke down every dose combination in the two leading combination classes, lipid and diabetes, by prescription volume. Doses are written the way product names write them: "ezetimibe 10mg + statin 5mg" becomes 10/5. The rosuvastatin combination sells in four doses, with 48.5% of prescriptions going to 10/5 alone and 33.2% to 10/10. The atorvastatin combination is even more concentrated: 10/10 alone takes 67.6% of prescriptions. The metformin-dapagliflozin diabetes combination is spread across nine doses. Same "combination drug," very different shapes of demand.

Now set that demand beside the products companies have registered. The atorvastatin combination has 96 products at 10/10, 97 at 10/20 and 66 at 10/40, spread almost evenly across the three doses. Judged by product count alone, the three doses look equally important. Yet two-thirds of prescriptions go to 10/10. The rosuvastatin side shows a mismatch as well: the 10/20 dose gets 10% of prescriptions but accounts for 31% of products, while the newest dose, 10/2.5, where demand is growing, accounts for just 6%. Prescriptions concentrate while products spread evenly: demand and supply have different shapes. In the chart below, the difference in width between the upper and lower bars is that gap.

Prescriptions pile onto one dose while products sit evenly across three, and demand data for every dose is published nationwide.

Figure 3 · Prescription share (top) vs registered-product share (bottom) by dose, Jun 2024. Differences in width are the gap

To put it in one number, take the share of prescriptions going to each pair's most-prescribed dose and subtract that dose's share of products. For the atorvastatin combination that is 67.6% against 37.1%, a gap of 30.5pp. Weighted by volume across both classes, the average gap is 18pp. The more heavily a dose is prescribed, the smaller its share of the product lineup.

To be precise about what this does and doesn't show: product counts are registered products, not production volume. How much each company makes of each product is not in this data. So the number says one thing only: the shape of the lineup differs from the shape of demand. What is oversupplied and what is short only becomes clear when each company adds its own production and shipment data. Public data shows that the mismatch exists, and no more.

Add time and one more thing appears. The rosuvastatin combination's lowest dose, 10/2.5, did not exist in 2019. It reached 8.5% in 2024 and 12% by March 2026. On the atorvastatin side, the low 10/5 dose entered only recently, at 5%. The patients who needed lower doses were there all along; the products arrived late. The signal had been there for years, too: a 2019 study found that 15.6% of prescriptions involved splitting tablets, which is what physicians and pharmacists do when the doses on sale don't match the doses patients need.

The industry very likely knew. But we could not find a public analysis that measures this mismatch nationally, dose by dose. Doing it required no new data, only joining two tables that are already public, one of prescriptions and one from the drug registry, on the nine-digit code.

05The yardsticks from this piece

To make these observations reusable for any drug class, here they are as yardsticks. We measured two things.

One is the dose gap: within an ingredient pair, the prescription share of the most-prescribed dose minus that dose's share of registered products. It is near zero when demand and the lineup have the same shape; the wider it gets, the thinner the lineup where demand is concentrated. Across the two classes here it averaged +18pp, and the atorvastatin combination came in at +30.5pp.

The other is the regional multiple: a region's share of prescriptions divided by its share of population. A value of 1.0 is proportional; the further from 1.0, the more that region has its own story. This is the yardstick that showed the rest of the country catching up to Seoul, with multiples converging toward 1.0.

The decision rule: if the dose gap is wide, if other signals point the same way (tablet-splitting, doses arriving late), and if the regional multiples are moving, that class's lineup and inventory allocation probably need to be redesigned around demand data. Public data alone can't settle it, though. Product counts are not production volumes, so the final call comes after adding each company's own production and shipment numbers.

06The money beside the data

The cost of leaving this data unused can be put in money. A HIRA research report (2018) estimated that duplicate prescriptions of the same ingredient waste ₩138.2 billion a year, 1.16% of prescription value. Drugs prescribed but never taken are estimated at ₩218 billion a year. Of leftover medicine, 55.2% ended up in the trash or down the drain, and in 94.4% of cases patients decided on their own whether to take it.

Infrastructure isn't the problem. EMR adoption among hospitals was already 93.9% in the 2020 survey, and institutions with certified EMR systems went from 41 in June 2020 to 4,052 by December 2024. The system that checks every outpatient prescription in real time for duplication and contraindications (DUR, drug utilization review) screened about 1.49 billion prescriptions in 2025. Prescription data in Korea flows in real time. What's missing is anyone turning that flow into demand and inventory planning, and that is where the money leaks.

There is one more gap. Waste on the patient side at least has official estimates. For inventory that hospitals and pharmacies throw away, we could not find a statistic at all. Official waste counts stop at the patient. Institutional stock is tracked only in each institution's own inventory records, and nobody adds it up.

07What this data cannot see

It sees only what insurance reimburses. The data captures ₩1.19 trillion a month, yet the Wegovy boom everyone has heard about registers as zero. Obesity drugs have no reimbursed products at all, and reimbursement claims for GLP-1 drugs, the class Wegovy belongs to, were zero at all three time points. A diabetes class that did win reimbursement, the SGLT2 inhibitors, shows up clearly, growing from ₩2.6 billion to ₩9.1 billion a month. The out-of-pocket market is outside this data's view.

These are prescriptions, not consumption. As the non-adherence figures in section 06 show, the two differ, so read prescription volume as demand with that margin in mind.

Diagnoses are primary diagnoses. That is the patient's main recorded condition, not the drug's indication. Use the distribution to understand the patient profile, not as indication statistics.

Regions are institution locations. They can differ from where patients live, and regions with many large hospitals look inflated. That is why we divide by population and read outflow and inflow pairs such as Sejong and Daejeon together.

Masking and lag. Cells with three or fewer manufacturers are masked, and the aggregates run months behind; the latest month in this piece is March 2026.

08What this piece is really saying

In the US we parsed physicians' notes to build prescription data, and had to stop. Korea publishes the same data by ingredient, dose, region and month, yet in ten years the API drew a little over two thousand applications and, as far as we could find, not one public analysis. Working with it directly showed three things: as combinations crossed half the market, dose demand for the best-selling drug and its product lineup diverged by an average of 18pp; low doses arrived years after the demand for them; and the regional multiples record the rest of the country catching up to Seoul over five years. The data was always there; nobody had looked. What the two yardsticks, dose gap and regional multiple, show for your own therapeutic class can be measured today from public data.

Method & limits

  • Sources: HIRA's drug-utilization open API (registered on Korea's open-data portal), covering nationwide claims for the top 100 therapeutic classes (ATC level 4) by reimbursed product count, at 2019-06, 2024-06 and 2026-03, plus district-level (298 official region codes) and ingredient-level sweeps for the lipid and diabetes combinations; 3,695 queries in total, counted on a dispensing basis, with all payer types summed. The product and dose axis comes from the reimbursed-drug ATC mapping list (as of 2025-06-30), 21,953 products.
  • The dose-safety account in section 01: drawn from the published literature, namely a randomized trial halted early for significantly higher mortality in its high-dose arm (CloroCovid-19, 2020). In most trials, efficacy differences were not significant. Personal experience supplies only the setting and timing.
  • Dose gap definition: within an ingredient pair, the volume share of the most-prescribed dose minus that dose's share of registered products. Product counts are registration counts, not production volumes, so the gap speaks only to the shape of the lineup.
  • Regional multiple definition: a province's share of prescription volume ÷ its share of resident population (Ministry of the Interior, same-month basis). Gwangju and South Jeolla are computed on their pre-2026 administrative boundaries.
  • Reproducibility: the figures in this piece are recomputed from the raw data by a verification script; application and repository counts are as of 2026-08-23.
